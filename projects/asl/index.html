ASL Fingerspelling Recognizer

A browser app that reads the American Sign Language alphabet from your webcam in about half a second per letter, and helps you learn it.

Before you try

Needs a webcam and one permission click. The video never leaves your device.

  • Works on desktop and on phones.
  • The camera opens only inside the app, never on this portfolio.

Why I built it

At a campus event I was in line next to a deaf man and couldn't communicate with him. Fingerspelling is the part of ASL used for names and words with no sign, and it's where many learners start. I wanted a tool that watches you sign and tells you, honestly, whether it's readable.

What it does

  • Recognises A–Z live, on desktop or phone, including J and Z (moving letters traced as a stroke).
  • Practice mode: a live "readable / not yet" meter with a correction guide drawn on your hand.
  • Challenge mode: a timed game with combos, Normal/Hard difficulty and look-alike letters.
  • Read mode: an animated hand spells a word and you type it, with a course that unlocks letters in a teaching order.
  • Spell mode: fingerspell continuously into a transcript; a beta mode segments it into words and speaks them.

How it works

  1. Track: Google's MediaPipe HandLandmarker finds 21 points on the hand in each frame.
  2. Normalise: points are recentred on the wrist and scaled to hand size, so distance and handedness don't matter.
  3. Classify: a k-nearest-neighbours classifier compares the shape to a labelled reference set built from 10,000+ images; small learned "refinement heads" break the ties k-NN gets wrong (M/N, D/O/C).
  4. Stabilise: a letter shows only after the same guess holds for a few frames.

Plain JavaScript: no ML framework at runtime, no build step, no network calls after load.

Hard parts

  • Look-alike letters: M, N, D, O and C differ by a finger or two, so I added targeted second-stage classifiers for those pairs.
  • Motion letters: J and Z are movements, not shapes, so they're detected from a traced stroke over time.
  • Honesty: I measured accuracy on held-out data and say plainly that it's a ceiling. It's mostly been tested by one signer, and it isn't native speed.

What I learned

How a real-time vision pipeline fits together end to end, why per-letter metrics matter more than one average, and how to run a disciplined test-and-fix loop with AI coding agents.

What's next

A sequence model that reads letter transitions (the way Google's fingerspelling research does) to reach real speed, digits 0–9, and testing with ASL educators and Deaf signers, ideally at RIT/NTID, to build it with them rather than for them.

Privacy

Everything runs in the browser. No video, images or hand data are uploaded, and there are no accounts or analytics.
